# One bale, five hands

We followed a single bale of cotton, lot 4471, from the field where it was picked to the shelf where a
shirt made from it was sold. The route took eleven months and passed through five companies. Each chapter
below is one pair of hands; the thread in the margin is the bale.

::::section{title="The field in Khorezm" id="field" thread="start"}
The bale was picked by machine in the second week of October, on a farm of 140 hectares that sells its
whole crop to one ginnery. The farm records the plot, the date and the moisture of every trailer, and the
trailer number stays on the bale as a paper tag.

Picking ran for nine days. Rain on the fourth day stopped the machines for a day and a half, and the
moisture of the trailers picked right after it was two points higher than the rest. Lot 4471 came from the
seventh day.
::::

::::section{title="Ginning and grading" id="gin" thread="pass"}
At the ginnery the seed cotton was cleaned, the seeds removed, and the lint pressed into bales of about
220 kilograms. A sample from each bale went to the grading room, where staple length, strength and
colour are measured by an instrument, not by eye.

Lot 4471 graded as middling, staple 1 3/32 inch. That grade decided its price and, as it turned out, the
mill it went to: the buyer wanted exactly this length for a fine-count yarn.
::::

::::section{title="The spinning mill" id="mill" thread="tangle"}
The mill blended our bale with thirty-nine others of the same grade, so from here on lot 4471 is not a
bale but a share of a lay-down. The mill keeps the list of bales in each lay-down, and that list is the
only reason the trail does not end at this door.

Spinning took three weeks. The yarn was wound onto cones, eighteen cones to a carton, and the cartons were
labelled with the lay-down number. Two of those cartons carried fibre from our bale.

- Lay-down: 40 bales, all middling.
- Yarn: 60s combed, ring spun.
- Cartons with fibre from lot 4471: 2 of 56.

The two cartons left the mill in the first week of January.
::::

::::section{title="Weaving and dyeing" id="weave" thread="pass"}
A weaving mill in the same region bought the two cartons with nine others and wove a poplin. The fabric
was dyed pale blue in a batch of 3,000 metres. The batch card names the yarn lots, so the link holds.

This is where most traces are lost. The dye house works on commission and keeps its batch cards for two
years; ours was in a folder that was due to be thrown away the following spring.
::::

::::section{title="Cutting, sewing and the shop" id="shop" thread="end"}
The garment factory cut 1,140 shirts from the batch. Each shirt carries a care label with a factory code
and a month, and nothing else that points back. We matched the code and the month to the cutting order,
and the cutting order to the batch.

The shirt we bought in April was one of them. The thread from the field reaches it, but only because every
company along the way kept one list it did not have to keep.
::::

::::section{title="What would break the trail" id="gaps"}
Three records carried the whole route: the trailer tag, the mill's lay-down list and the dye house's
batch card. Lose any one and the bale is untraceable past that point, however good the other records are.
::::
